02My role

The idea, the product, the first codebase, and the parts of the next two that people touch

Initiative

Named the risk in the team's scattered AI use, defined the product, and built it without a brief. When the company committed to new markets, I pivoted the same tool into a localization engine instead of starting a second product.

The TypeScript and Lit app, the Cloudflare Worker and its Azure client, the pipeline and its CLI workflow, and 221 Vitest tests. 493 of the repo's 524 commits; one engineer contributed 21.

v2With engineering

Engineering rebuilt the pipeline in Python on Azure, porting v1's quality gates file by file. I wrote the review-console spec from v1's four failures, prototyped it, then built the review API and the React console. 113 of the repo's 717 commits.

The WYSIWYG editor and its round-trip gate, inline flags, the localizer workspace, the admin overview, view-as, server-side drafts, and the content-generation workflow. Engineers wired the always-gated publish flow and the scope model. 81 commits.

Grounding and guardrails

Nothing is trained. Everything is prompted, retrieved from a per-locale knowledge base, verified by a separate pass, and approved by a person. The voice, compliance, and imagery rubrics are mine.

03Where it started

Everyone had an AI tool. None of them sounded like us.

AI became a daily habit across the content team, each writer on a personal subscription with no shared rules. The drafts came fast, drifted from the company's voice, and nothing recorded which claims had been checked. In a business built on financial trust, that is a liability, and nobody owned it.

The question I asked

What if the tool already knew what our content looked like, instead of a blank page with a model bolted on?

Voice pillars, compliance rules, and the schema for every content type, applied by default. People stop reaching for tools we cannot control because the sanctioned one is better.

Before

Personal subscriptions. Off-brand drafts. No record of what was checked. One more review round for every piece.

After v1

One studio. Company voice and compliance by default. Unsourced claims flagged beside the editor. Drafts in minutes, with a publish-readiness checklist.

04v1Built alone · Apr – May 2026

Four weeks from first commit to launch QA, one designer, no engineering.

I built it with Claude Code as the pair and the Anthropic API inside the product. The first working version took two weeks. The rest was tuning until every piece held up on real content.

The author picks a content type, a locale, and an audience, then writes a topic. Every request carries the voice pillars, the compliance rules, and the schema for that type. Thirteen content types, from articles and quizzes to life events and calculators.

Uncertainty is shown, not hidden. The model must cite a source it consulted or flag the claim. Flagged claims appear beside the editor, each with the figure to confirm and where to confirm it.

One Worker holds every secret. The browser never sees the API key. A Cloudflare Worker proxies the model and fronts Azure Blob Storage, the source of truth for every entry, variant, and flag. One write path means one place to enforce the rules.

05v1Going global · May – Jun 2026

"Contribute to your 401(k)" in French is still wrong when every word is correct.

Then the company set its sights on every market. The library assumed a US reader on every page: 401(k)s, HSAs, the IRS. Vendor translation runs weeks per asset and gives you a literal rendering of advice that does not apply. The traditional path was one in-country localizer per market, for a 592-piece library.

The second question

What if localizing meant re-authoring the piece for that market, with a person signing off, instead of translating it?

Swap the retirement system, the tax authority, the register, and the examples. Keep the coach's voice. Flag anything the model cannot source, and let a native localizer have the final say.

06Decisions and tradeoffs

Four calls I would make again

A per-locale wiki instead of open-web search

Not taken: letting the model research each market live on the web at draft time.

Why: financial facts have to be ours. A curated wiki is slower to build, but every retirement system and tax authority the model cites is something a person vetted. Web search feeds the wiki; it does not bypass it.

Five model calls per article, not one

Not taken: the original one-pass pipeline, which cost about a fifth as much.

Why: one-pass output kept US framing and US-only links that no automated check could see. Gate, research, draft, verify, and save took an article to roughly 335k tokens, which forced targeted re-runs instead of full rewrites.

The score routes; it does not gate

Not taken: publishing anything above a confidence threshold.

Why: the confidence number is the model grading its own output. Useful for deciding what a reviewer sees first, useless as a guarantee. There is no threshold in the code, on purpose.

Approved is a state automation cannot cross

Not taken: letting a batch run refresh every variant in its scope, which is simpler and what the first version did.

Why: a person's judgment is the most expensive thing in the system. Runs skip approved work by default, and re-localizing an approved piece is a named, explicit request. My call, dated August 5, 2026.

07The turning point · Jun – Aug 2026

It worked. Then it hit the limits of a tool one person built.

By August the library held more than eight thousand localized variants and two localizers were working through them. The tool that got us there could not carry the next stage, and I wrote down why. These four failures open the spec for the next version.

01 · Lost editsA re-run was a rewrite, not an edit

A 25-article Mexico batch replaced variants a localizer had already corrected, and the log said success. On three reviewed articles, none of the reviewed sentences survived: 26 of 26, 32 of 32, 32 of 32. The only signal was a localizer recognizing text she had already fixed.

02 · Flags hard to act onReviewers did not know what to look at

Flags lived in a separate tab, carried machine names, and offered no action. One Bulgarian run produced 630 flags on 445 articles, 78% of them "check this number". Counting flags as a negative would have told us the worse pipeline was better.

03 · No audit trailNo identity, no history

A first-visit name prompt stored in the browser stood in for sign-in. Nobody could say who approved a variant, who dismissed a flag, or why. An append-only review log came late and covered only part of it.

04 · Slow at scaleEvery request parsed a 3.5 MB index

The Worker hit its CPU limit reading the whole library index on every call. Post-passes touched the entire corpus on every batch. The CLI and in-app pipelines had also drifted into two implementations.

Also found and fixed in v1: 5,455 published variants carried English titles and slugs on localized bodies; US-only links were invisible to every automated check until a verify stage and a link check landed (51 dangling calls to action across 27 locales on the first scan); a worker pool derived five different locale briefs per batch until a single-flight lock fixed it; and a swallowed parse error left jobs at "running" forever.

08v2The review console · Aug – Oct 2026

Engineering rebuilt the pipeline in Python. I designed and built the console the localizers work in.

The company moved localization onto its workflow platform: Python workers on Azure, a queue-driven orchestrator, a document store for state. The engineers ported v1's quality gates file by file. My part was everything a person touches: the spec, an HTML prototype, then the review API and the React console, twenty commits in the first two days.

Lost editsThe reviewer's copy is its own field

Only Approve writes the body that publishes. Every save carries a version check, so a stale save is refused instead of winning. Full versions are kept for a year, with Restore, and autosave fires 1.5 seconds after typing stops.

Flags hard to act onA flag rail in the margin

"Needs your call" and "Already handled", with plain labels: verification:freshness became "Check this number". Three actions: Apply when a suggestion exists, Fix myself, Dismiss with a reason. Apply is refused for flags that point at the English source, because that fix belongs upstream.

No audit trailRoles enforced on the server

A localizer is locked to their locale; only a publisher can release. Each article carries a 50-entry revision log, and every flag carries a resolution block in the same shape the self-heal stage writes, so a human fix and a machine fix read the same way.

Slow at scaleLoad only what is on screen

Stored flag counts, queue queries that load only the listed fields, 50 per page. Batch calls for heavy work, single calls for cheap judgments. Re-runs skip unchanged work and reuse market-level verdicts.

The pipeline, rebuilt: eighteen stages, one human gate

Atlas's passes became a state machine. Workers advance the next stage as soon as they finish; a scheduled sweep retries stale stages and never dispatches one twice. Engineering owns the pipeline; the console and the review API are mine.

1–2Judge

Turn reviewer verdicts into a work list. Judge each article's fit per market, and skip anything a reviewer already touched.

3–7Author

Re-author into local English with web search, find action items with no article and write them, translate with the English kept beside it, and sync edits between languages.

8–9Picture

Judge the existing header image for the market, then pick a licensed photo or generate one.

10–13Gate

Run the deterministic gates and stage for review with flags attached. A self-heal stage fixes what is mechanical and never approves.

14–15People

Localizer review, then publisher release. The only stages the run does not wait for.

16–18Serve

Embed published articles for search, turn on routing for the locale, and run the Q&A and life-event tracks in parallel.

Forty-three locale grounding files and 22 markets configured for batch generation.

09v3Inside the admin platform · Aug – Oct 2026

Then it moved into the platform localizers already use, with an editor for people who do not write markup.

The console proved the model. The company wanted it where every other admin task lives, behind the same sign-in, with the same roles. I started that build the day after the v2 console merged.

One text box, WYSIWYG, with a round-trip gate. The rich editor opens only if it can reproduce the article byte for byte; otherwise the page falls back to a plain source view rather than silently reformatting a localizer's work. Tested against 887 real article bodies.

Flags live in the text. Inline highlights link both ways with the cards in the rail. The English companion sits beside the localized pane, and a third pane compares to the US source on demand.

Drafts live on the server, and a conflict keeps your edits. On a conflict the page shows the other writer's version beside yours instead of discarding either.

Routing is the locale, not a person. A localizer covers one market and sees only it. An admin sees every market as a matrix and can view the product as any localizer's seat, with publishing kept out of that seat.

The interface hides; the server enforces. Single sign-on, scopes resolved server-side, and a review API that checks the role on every request. Publishing and deletion are publisher-gated with a count guard.

Next: the system proposes what to write

Localizing the US library only gets a market so far. The generation workflow reads a locale's library against a prompt, shows where coverage is thin, proposes articles, lists what already exists in English to localize instead, and writes the chosen set through the same pipeline. Up to 200 in one batch, every piece landing in review.

Status: designed and running on demo data in the admin platform. The batch backend is a proposed contract; the single-article generator is live on the pipeline platform.

10Designing the AI, not just the screens

A rubric I wrote in v1, and a human verdict that outranks the model in v2

A localized article with a US-coded photo undoes the text. So the header image gets the same treatment as the words: a cultural read, a brief, a check, and a person.

Where AI did the work, and where I said no. The verify stage is a model call with a typed vocabulary of failures and a pass-or-fail verdict that triggers a redo, written with Claude Code. What I did not accept: the model's own confidence as a quality signal, and fluent one-pass drafts that still carried US assumptions.

How it was checked. 221 Vitest tests cover the gate, the policy, the rewrite, and the index logic. The link check ran against 8,120 real variants first. Of 75 UK articles the fit check dropped, 13 had a UK analogue and were rewritten; 62 could not honestly exist there.

What the humans still catch. A figure tied only to a secondary source. A slug left in English. A register slip from usted to tú. The system puts those in front of the right person with the context to decide.

12What I would do differently

Three things I have since done or scoped

Measure review time from day one

No version has a review-time or throughput metric. The tracker that would have measured it was designed and never built. "Slow at scale" should have been a number, not a feeling.

One implementation, not two

v1 grew a CLI pipeline and an in-app pipeline that drifted apart. v2 fixed it by making the pipeline one codebase owned by engineering, which I should have pushed for before the hand-off.

Identity before scale

A browser name prompt was fine for two localizers and wrong for twenty-five markets. Next time the audit trail ships with the first Approve button.

I saw a gap nobody owned, built the first answer alone, then wrote down where it failed and rebuilt it twice with engineers until it was part of the company's platform. The product shows what AI unlocks for a content team. The way it was built shows the same for design.
