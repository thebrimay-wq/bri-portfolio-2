My role: I found the gap, designed the Studio, and coded the first version (493 of its 524 commits). Then I led the localizers and built the review screens while engineering rebuilt the engine with me. Team: our principal engineer, two engineers, and 35 localizers.

01 · The gap

Everyone was writing with their own AI

When AI tools arrived, our writers started drafting on personal accounts. Each tool had its own voice, nobody checked its sources, and our library stopped sounding like one company.

What I saw

A large, growing library with no single Financial Finesse voice, written on tools the company didn't own or review.

What it needed

Reins. One tool, owned by the company, that every writer could use and every reviewer could trust.

What I did

Nobody asked me to. I built it in my free time, and it was how I learned Claude Code.

02 · The first version

One studio that writes like us

I gave the AI what a good writer at Financial Finesse knows: our voice, taken from our own library, every content type and audience we write for, and a short list of sources it may use.

Sources it may use

Government, education, and established financial sites. Opinion pieces are off the list, and every fact traces back to one of them.

A screen that fits the format

Writers pick from dropdowns and type a topic. The layout changes with the choice, because a quiz reads differently from an article.

Edit it, or ask for a change

Rewrite any line by hand, or select a passage and tell the AI what you want: shorter, more conversational, bullets, anything.

Flags where it's unsure

When the AI doubts a fact, it highlights the passage and raises a flag. A reviewer fixes it, approves it, or dismisses it.

Ready for the CMS

Every draft arrives with its metadata, slug, and the fields our CMS needs, so nobody retypes them.

Built with Claude Code, versioned on GitHub, hosted on Vercel behind a password. The code is private to Financial Finesse.

04 · The pipeline

Eight stages between a US article and a new country

I researched what each market needs and built the pipeline every article runs through. Every stage pulls from reputable sources and cites them.

StageWhat happens
1 · Learn the marketResearch the country's taxes, pensions, regulators, currency, and terms (401(k) becomes RRSP), then fact-check all of it.
2 · Judge each articleKeep, rewrite, or drop. Does the topic exist there, and does it matter?
3 · LocalizeRewrite the kept articles with local rules, figures, and examples.
4 · Fill the gapsFind topics the market needs that no article covers, and write them.
5 · TranslatePut everything into the local language, with the English version alongside.
6 · Fit the imagesKeep the images that suit the market and replace the rest.
7 · Quality checkFix formatting, strip US-only links and terms, and flag anything a person should look at.
8 · Human reviewA localizer reviews and approves each article. Nothing goes live until a person releases it.

Stages 3, 4, and 6 run at the same time. Aimee's Q&A and our life events get their own judge, localize, and review steps. When the pipeline judged the UK, 62 of the 75 articles it held back didn't apply there.

300 core articles

We picked 300 articles for the global offering. The Studio localized them, then a gap analysis found what each country was missing and wrote it, without duplicating anything we had.

06 · People in the loop

I didn't want AI to be the last one to read it

The team hired localizers in all 25 countries, and I lead them. Each one reviews every article for their country, starting with the ones that carry the most flags.

Fix it in the text

Localizers act on each flag with Apply, Fix myself, or Dismiss, then read the whole piece and change anything else it needs.

One review, not two

Every non-English article carries an English version that updates as the localizer edits, so nobody reviews the same article twice.

Their notes tune the pipeline

We meet every week. When a localizer sees the same mistake across many articles, I fix it in the pipeline, and it stops showing up in every market.

07 · What broke

It worked, until it didn't

At 25 countries, a tool one person built hit its limits. I wrote down four problems, joined the company's codebase, and rebuilt the Studio inside our CMS in a week.

01 · Lost editsEdits are safe now

A re-run replaced work a localizer had corrected. On three reviewed articles, none of the reviewed sentences survived. Now a reviewer's text saves on its own, and an out-of-date save can't overwrite it.

02 · Flags nobody could act onPlain words, clear actions

One Bulgarian run raised 630 flags on 445 articles in a tab with no buttons. Now each flag says what's wrong, like "Check this number", and offers a fix.

03 · No audit trailOne sign-in, real roles

A name typed into the browser stood in for sign-in, so nobody could say who approved what. Now reviewers use their work sign-in and see only their country.

04 · Slow at scaleFast with a big library

Pages timed out as the library grew. The rebuild stores flag counts and loads 50 rows at a time.

Our principal engineer and I also built a console for the pipeline, so we can automate runs and push updates.

08 · Decisions

Four calls I'd make again

We never trained a model. The AI writes from our voice guide, sources we approve, and each country's facts, and a person approves what it writes.

Cite it or flag it

The AI researches from sources we approve and cites them. Anything it can't source becomes a flag for a person.

Eight stages, not one fast pass

One quick pass kept US habits no check could catch. Eight stages cost more and catch them.

The AI's score doesn't decide what ships

It decides what a reviewer sees first. There's no auto-publish setting, on purpose.

Approved means approved

Once a person approves an article, the tool won't touch it again unless someone asks for that article by name.

Next: a wiki of everything we know
