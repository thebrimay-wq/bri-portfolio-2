Where it startedEarly 2026

Everyone had an AI tool. None of them sounded like us.

AI became a daily habit across the content team, each writer on a personal subscription with no shared rules. The drafts came fast, drifted from the company's voice, and nothing recorded which claims had been checked. In a business built on financial trust, that is a liability, and I could see nobody owned it.

The question I asked

What if the tool already knew what our content looked like, instead of a blank page with a model bolted on?

Voice pillars, compliance rules, and the schema for every content type applied by default, so every draft comes back sounding like the company. People stop reaching for tools we cannot control because the sanctioned one is better.

Before

Personal subscriptions. Off-brand drafts. No record of what was checked. One more review round for every piece.

After v1

One studio. Company voice and compliance by default. Unsourced claims flagged beside the editor. Drafts in minutes, with a publish-readiness checklist.

v1Global Content Studio · built solo · Apr – May 2026

Four weeks from first commit to launch QA, one designer, no engineering.

I built it with Claude Code as the pair and the Anthropic API inside the product. The first working version took two weeks. The rest was tuning until every piece held up on real content.

01

The author picks a content type, a locale, and an audience, then writes a topic. Every request carries the voice pillars, the compliance rules, and the schema for that content type. Thirteen content types, from articles and quizzes to life events and calculators.

02

Uncertainty is shown, not hidden. The model must cite a source it consulted or flag the claim. Flagged claims appear in an accuracy-check panel beside the editor, each with the figure to confirm and where to confirm it.

03

One Worker holds every secret. The browser never sees the API key. A Cloudflare Worker proxies the model and fronts Azure Blob Storage, which is the source of truth for every entry, variant, and flag. One write path means one place to enforce the rules.

v1The turn · May 2026

"Contribute to your 401(k)" in French is still wrong when every word is correct.

Then the company set its sights on every market. The library assumed a US reader on every page: 401(k)s, HSAs, the IRS. Vendor translation runs weeks per asset and gives you a literal rendering of advice that does not apply. The traditional path was one in-country localizer per market, recruited and managed, for a 592-piece library.

The second question

What if localizing meant re-authoring the piece for that market, with a person signing off, instead of translating it?

Swap the retirement system, the tax authority, the register, and the examples. Keep the coach's voice. Flag anything the model cannot source, and let a native localizer have the final say.

Decisions and tradeoffs

Four calls I would make again

A per-locale wiki instead of open-web search

Not taken: letting the model research each market live on the web at draft time.

Why: financial facts have to be ours. A curated wiki is slower to build, one locale at a time, but every retirement system and tax authority the model cites is something a person vetted. Web search still runs in the research stage; it feeds the wiki, it does not bypass it.

Five model calls per article, not one

Not taken: the original one-pass pipeline, which cost about a fifth as much.

Why: one-pass output kept US framing and US-only links that no automated check could see. Splitting into gate, research, draft, independent verify, and save took an article to roughly 335k tokens. That cost is what forced the skip-approved default and targeted re-runs instead of full-corpus rewrites.

The score routes; it does not gate

Not taken: publishing anything above a confidence threshold.

Why: the confidence number is the model grading its own output. It is useful for deciding what a reviewer sees first and useless as a guarantee. The gate is a separate verify pass with typed failure reasons, deterministic checks, and a person. There is no threshold in the code, on purpose.

Approved is a state automation cannot cross

Not taken: letting a batch run refresh every variant in its scope, which is simpler and what the first version did.

Why: a run once consumed articles a localizer had already approved and reported "25 written, 0 errors". A person's judgment is the most expensive thing in the system. Runs now skip approved work by default, and re-localizing an approved piece is a named, explicit request. That decision is dated August 5, 2026, and it is mine.

The turning point · Jun – Aug 2026

It worked. Then it hit the limits of a tool one person built.

By August the library held more than eight thousand localized variants and two localizers were working through them. The tool that got us there could not carry the next stage, and I wrote down why. These four failures open the spec for the next version, in my words: the previous version failed on these four.

01 · Lost edits

A re-run was a rewrite, not an edit

A 25-article Mexico batch replaced variants a localizer had already corrected. Measured on three reviewed articles, none of the reviewed sentences survived: 26 of 26, 32 of 32, 32 of 32. The log said success. The only signal was a localizer recognizing text she had already fixed.

02 · Flags hard to act on

Reviewers did not know what to look at

Flags lived in a separate tab, carried machine names, and offered no action. A single Bulgarian run produced 630 flags on 445 articles, 78% of them "check this number". Counting flags as a negative would have told us the worse pipeline was better.

03 · No audit trail

No identity, no history

A first-visit name prompt stored in the browser stood in for sign-in. Nobody could say who approved a variant, who dismissed a flag, or why. An append-only review log came late and covered only part of it.

04 · Slow at scale

Every request parsed a 3.5 MB index

The Worker hit its CPU limit reading the whole library index on every call. Post-passes touched the entire corpus on every batch, and a setup step emitted all 560 articles for a 3-article run. The CLI and in-app pipelines had also drifted into two implementations.

Also found and fixed in v1 before the hand-off: 5,455 published variants carried English titles and slugs on localized bodies, caught in an August audit of the live data; US-only links were invisible to every automated check until the verify stage and a deterministic link check landed (51 dangling calls to action across 27 locales and 8,120 variants on the first scan); the 5-worker pool derived five different locale briefs per batch until a keyed single-flight lock fixed it; and a swallowed parse error left jobs at "running" forever until unparseable model output failed loudly with a retry.
v2The review console on the pipeline platform · Aug – Oct 2026

Engineering rebuilt the pipeline in Python. I designed and built the console the localizers work in.

The company moved localization onto its workflow platform: Python workers on Azure, a queue-driven orchestrator, a document store for state, a vector store for search. The engineers ported v1's quality gates file by file. My part was everything a person touches: the spec written from the four failures, an HTML prototype, then the review API and the React console, twenty commits in the first two days.

01 · Lost edits on re-runA re-run overwrote human work and reported success.
→

The reviewer's copy is its own field, and only Approve writes the body that publishes. Every save carries a version check; a stale save is refused instead of winning. Full versions are kept for a year, with Restore. Autosave fires 1.5 seconds after typing stops and each draft is tagged to its article, after a bug where one article's text landed on another.

02 · Flags hard to act onMachine names in a separate tab, no action to take.
→

A flag rail in the margin, split into "Needs your call" and "Already handled", with plain labels. verification:freshness became "Check this number". Three actions: Apply when a suggestion exists, Fix myself, Dismiss with a reason. Apply is refused for flags that point at the English source, because the fix belongs upstream.

03 · No audit trailA name in a browser prompt stood in for identity.
→

Roles on the company identity provider, enforced on the server for every route. A localizer is locked to their locale; only a publisher can release. Each article carries a review block (assignee, last editor, edit count, approver) and a 50-entry revision log, and every flag carries a resolution block in the same shape the self-heal stage writes, so a human fix and a machine fix read the same way. The dismiss reason is the audit trail.

04 · Slow at scaleWhole-index reads, whole-corpus post-passes.
→

Stored flag counts, queue queries that load only the listed fields, 50 per page. Batch calls for heavy work, single calls for cheap judgments. Re-runs skip unchanged work and reuse market-level verdicts. A paging bug that made articles past the first 50 unreachable (24 in one locale, 111 in another) is fixed and tested.

The pipeline, rebuilt: eighteen stages, one human gate

Atlas's passes became a state machine on Azure. Workers advance the next stage as soon as they finish; a scheduled sweep retries stale stages with backoff and never dispatches a stage twice. The human review stage is the only one the run does not wait for.

Judge

  1. 1Rework scan turns reviewer verdicts into a work list
  2. 2Evaluate suitability per market; skips what a reviewer touched

Author

  1. 3Localize re-authors into local English with web search
  2. 4Gap scan finds action items with no article
  3. 5Author writes the missing pieces
  4. 6Translate renders the target language; English kept beside it
  5. 7Pair sync carries edits between languages, flags conflicts

Picture

  1. 8Image evaluate judges the existing header image
  2. 9Images picks a licensed photo or generates one

Gate

  1. 10Stage runs the deterministic gates, writes for-review docs and flags
  2. 11Proposals give every action item a resource or a reason
  3. 12Heal fixes what is mechanical; never approves
  4. 13Link localize

People

  1. 14Localizer review in the console
  2. 15Publisher release

Serve

  1. 16Embed published articles for search
  2. 17Enable routing for the locale
  3. 18Q&A and life-event tracks run in parallel

Forty-three locale grounding files, 22 markets configured for batch generation, and a self-heal stage that writes the same resolution block a human does. Engineering owns the pipeline; the console, the review API, and the batch-generation modes added in October are mine.

v3Global Content Review in the admin platform · Aug – Oct 2026

Then it moved into the platform localizers already use, with an editor for people who do not write markup.

The review console proved the model. The company wanted it where every other admin task lives, behind the same sign-in, with the same roles. I started that build the day after the v2 console merged, and it became the surface the localizers work in.

01

One text box, WYSIWYG, with a round-trip gate. The rich editor opens only if it can reproduce the article byte for byte; otherwise the page falls back to a plain source view rather than silently reformatting a localizer's work. The gate is tested against 887 real article bodies.

02

Flags live in the text. Inline decorations link both ways with the cards in the rail, so "find in text" is one click. The English companion sits beside the localized pane, and a third pane compares to the US source on demand.

03

Drafts live on the server, and a conflict keeps your edits. Every save carries a version check and a merge base. On a conflict the page shows the other writer's version beside yours instead of discarding either. An unsaved-edits guard stops accidental navigation.

04

Routing is the locale, not a person. A localizer covers exactly one market and sees only it; an admin sees every staged locale as a matrix and can view the product as any localizer's seat, with publishing kept out of that seat. Editing presence is a label for coordination, never a lock.

05

Enforcement moved to the server. Single sign-on through the company identity provider, scopes resolved server-side, and a review API that filters locales and checks the role on every request. The interface only ever hides; the server enforces. Publishing and deletion are publisher-gated with a count guard and a version check.

Next: the system proposes what to write

Localizing the US library only gets a market so far. The generation workflow reads a locale's library against a prompt, reports where coverage is thin, proposes articles, lists what already exists in English to localize instead, and writes the chosen set through the same pipeline so every piece lands in review. Up to 200 in one batch, three in flight per locale, with the guardrails printed on the page: financial wellness only, written as a coach, compliant by default, true in this market, and a person always reviews it.

Status: designed and running on demo data in the admin platform; the batch backend is a proposed contract, and the single-article generator is live on the pipeline platform.
Designing the AI, not just the screens

The image engine: a rubric I wrote in v1, and a human verdict that outranks the model in v2

A localized article with a US-coded photo undoes the text. So the header image gets the same treatment as the words: a cultural read, a brief, a check, and a person.

01

Where AI did the work, and where I said no. The verify stage is a model call with a typed vocabulary of failures and a pass-or-fail verdict that triggers a redo. The stage, its schema, and the fan-out workflow were written with Claude Code. What I did not accept: the model's own confidence as a quality signal, since it is self-grading; and fluent one-pass drafts that still carried US assumptions, which are exactly what the stage exists to fail.

02

How it was checked. Vitest covers the gate, the policy, the rewrite, and the index logic (221 tests, September 2026). The link check ran against real data first: 51 dangling calls to action across 27 locales and 8,120 variants. The fit check was measured the same way: of 75 UK articles it dropped, 13 had a UK analogue and were rewritten; 62 could not honestly exist there. The v3 editor's round-trip gate runs against 887 real bodies. Then the localizers reviewed the output, which is the check that counts.

03

What the humans still catch. A figure tied only to a secondary source. A slug left in English. A register slip from usted to tú. The system exists to put those in front of the right person with the context to decide, not to decide for them.

What I would do differently

Three things, each of which I have since done or scoped

Measure review time from day one

No version has a review-time or throughput metric. The Tracker that would have measured it was designed for v2 and never built. It is the first thing I would ship next, because "slow at scale" should have been a number, not a feeling.

One implementation, not two

v1 grew a CLI pipeline and an in-app pipeline that drifted apart. v2 fixed it by making the pipeline someone else's single codebase, which is the right answer and one I should have reached before the hand-off.

Identity before scale

A browser name prompt was fine for two localizers and wrong for twenty-five markets. v2 put roles on the identity provider on its first day; v3 moved every check to the server. Next time the audit trail ships with the first approve button.

Final takeaway

I saw a gap nobody owned, built the first answer alone, and then did the harder thing: wrote down where it failed and rebuilt it twice with engineers until it was part of the company's platform. The product proves what AI unlocks for a content team. The way it was built proves the same thing for design.
