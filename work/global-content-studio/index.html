I built an AI writing tool. It became the system that helped us go global.

What began as a problem I noticed inside Financial Finesse became a working AI-native product, a localization engine, and a new capability for the whole business. I identified the opportunity, taught myself Claude Code, designed and shipped the first version, and helped evolve it into a system that adapts financial education for markets around the world. It changed what the business could do, and it changed how I design.

01 · The problem

Everyone was using AI. Nobody was using it the same way.

At Financial Finesse, content is the center of the product: articles, quizzes, checklists, and personalized guidance that help people make financial decisions. As generative AI became accessible, our teams started using it on their own. Different accounts, different prompts, different sources, different results.

What I saw

Content came faster, but it didn't always match our brand or editorial standards. In personal finance, information that only sounds credible isn't good enough.

The opportunity

It wasn't on our roadmap. We didn't need another AI writer. We needed a system people could trust.

The challenge

Make AI useful enough to speed up content, structured enough to keep it consistent, and transparent enough that our team stays in control.

02 · Taking initiative

Nobody assigned me this problem. I decided to build the solution.

I'd spent years designing products and working with engineers to bring them to life. I wanted to close the gap between having an idea and putting a working product in someone's hands. Claude Code had just become available, so I started building on my own time.

I started with the library, not screens

I needed to understand how our company wrote, what made each content type different, which audiences we served, and what rules kept it all consistent.

The foundation

I defined the content structures, writing standards, and source constraints that became the base of the system: our voice from our own library, every content type and audience, and a short list of government, education, and reputable financial sources the AI may use.

Then I built it

I designed and developed the first working application, four weeks from first commit to launch QA. What usually needs a handoff from design to engineering became something I could demonstrate. Instead of pitching an idea, I put a working product in front of the people it would help.

Built with Claude Code, versioned on GitHub, hosted on Vercel behind a password. The code is private to Financial Finesse.

03 · The product

Designing AI around how people actually work

I didn't want another empty chat box that depends on every employee writing the perfect prompt. I wanted AI to fit the work. That led to three product decisions.

01Structure where consistency matters

Guided inputs for content type, audience, locale, and topic. An article and a quiz have different requirements, so the interface adapts the editing experience to each. High-quality output becomes repeatable instead of depending on individual prompting skill.

02Direct control where precision matters

AI writes the first draft. Writers rewrite a passage, change the tone, or restructure one section without starting over. AI speeds up the work; the writer keeps ownership.

03Visible uncertainty where trust matters

Financial guidance has real consequences. The experience surfaces questionable information as flags in the text, and reviewers investigate and correct it. Confidence scores are signals for what needs attention, never proof of accuracy. Publication stays a human decision.

My design principle: AI does the repetitive work. People stay in control of consequential decisions.

04 · The turning point

Then I realized we weren't just solving content creation

Once the first version worked, I started generating content in other languages. Around the same time, our CEO had been talking about taking the company global. We already had a deep financial wellness library. What if we could adapt that knowledge for new markets?

Translation changes the language

Localization changes the meaning to fit the market. Retirement systems, taxes, regulations, currencies, terminology, and cultural expectations vary by country.

A 401(k) article can't be translated for Canada

First you decide whether the concept applies. Then you learn the local system and adapt the guidance. Sometimes an article needs a rewrite, sometimes a new topic, and sometimes the responsible choice is not to publish it at all.

05 · The system

To scale globally, we had to design for local truth

One question shaped the workflow: how do we make content accurate and relevant for a specific market, not just readable in another language? The answer was Atlas, an eight-stage localization pipeline. Every stage pulls from reputable sources and cites them.

StageWhat happens
1 · Learn the marketResearch the country's taxes, pensions, regulators, currency, and terms (401(k) becomes RRSP), then fact-check all of it.
2 · Judge each articleKeep, rewrite, or drop. Does the topic exist there, and does it matter?
3 · LocalizeRewrite the kept articles with local rules, figures, and examples.
4 · Fill the gapsFind topics the market needs that no article covers, and write them.
5 · TranslatePut everything into the local language, with the English version alongside.
6 · Fit the imagesKeep the images that suit the market and replace the rest.
7 · Quality checkFix formatting, strip US-only links and terms, and flag anything a person should look at.
8 · Human reviewA localizer reviews and approves each article. Nothing goes live until a person releases it.

Stages 3, 4, and 6 run at the same time. Aimee's Q&A and our life events get their own judge, localize, and review steps. When the pipeline judged the UK, 62 of the 75 articles it held back didn't apply there.

Relevance before generation

The system decides whether the original topic makes sense for the market. It keeps, rewrites, or rejects content instead of localizing everything.

Grounding before confidence

The model works from our content standards and curated, market-specific reference material. A separate verification pass and automated checks catch problems a confidence score can't.

Human approval before publication

Local reviewers make the final decision. The system helps them spend their expertise where it matters most.

300 core articles

We picked 300 articles for the global offering. The Studio localized them, then a gap analysis found what each country was missing and wrote it, without duplicating anything we had.

I showed our CEO

She was ecstatic. Going global became the company's focus right away, and the Studio became how we'd do it. This wasn't a prompt wrapped in an interface. It was a repeatable workflow built around decisions, dependencies, verification, and human control.

06 · Learning from real people

The system improved when people started using it

As the localization effort grew, the company hired local reviewers who understood the financial realities of their markets, 35 localizers in 26 countries. I lead their review workflow.

Problems first

The queue puts the most-flagged content first. Reviewers inspect the whole article, correct it inline with Apply, Fix myself, or Dismiss, and approve it for publication.

One review, not two

Every non-English article carries an English version that updates as the localizer edits, so nobody reviews the same article twice.

Review is how the system learns

Their feedback showed that human review couldn't be a cleanup step at the end. We meet every week, and when a localizer sees the same mistake across many articles, I fix the cause in the pipeline instead of letting them fix it article after article.

The best automation wasn't the one that removed people. It was the one that made their expertise count for more.

07 · What broke

Building fast exposed problems a prototype can't predict

Getting to working software quickly was valuable. Shipping it into real workflows exposed weaknesses that needed better product and engineering decisions.

Problem 1AI was missing context people understood

Some localized articles kept links to US-only content. The writing looked local while sending readers somewhere irrelevant. What changed: a separate verification pass and deterministic link checks find and fix them. What I learned: a convincing output isn't a complete experience. Validation has to cover what happens beyond the generated text.

Problem 2Automation overwrote human expertise

During a batch re-run, reviewed content lost its human corrections. On three reviewed articles, none of the reviewed sentences survived. What changed: approved content is protected by default, with explicit controls for intentional re-localization. What I learned: AI automation needs boundaries that respect the user's ownership of the work.

Problem 3The prototype lived outside our platform

Starting on my own let me learn and demonstrate the idea fast, but it meant integration work once the app became a business capability. A name typed into the browser stood in for sign-in, and pages timed out as the library grew. What changed: I joined the company's codebase and rebuilt the Studio inside our CMS in a week, with real sign-in, roles, and an audit trail. What I learned: prototype speed matters, and so does choosing foundations that can carry the next stage.

08 · From product to platform

What started as my prototype became part of the platform

As its value became clear, I moved the application into our existing systems and worked with engineering on the larger localization workflow. My role changed with the product.

Hands on the code

I was no longer designing screens and handing them off. I contribute to the repositories, build production interfaces, write tests, and work on the systems behind the experience. Our principal engineer and I built a console for the pipeline so we can automate runs and push updates.

Beyond articles

Engineering adopted the pipeline fast. It now localizes every part of the Hub, from Aimee, our AI coach, to the logic and scoring behind our financial wellness assessment. A full Hub for a new country takes a week or less.

A new role for me

I became the company's AI champion, moved to the AI team, and spoke at our offsite about building with AI. Part of my job now is building internal tools that save our teams time and money.

Next: a wiki of everything we know

I'm working with our AI team on a wiki of every article we've written, with a baseline for each country. It's already the pipeline's first research step, before any web search. Localizers will review the wiki itself, edited articles flow back into it, and a crawler will update facts that change, like taxes and laws. In progress.

We weren't just scaling content. We were building a reusable capability for international expansion.

09 · The outcome

One small experiment changed what the business could pursue

International content production became a repeatable workflow instead of a market-by-market effort from scratch. We're localizing for 70 more countries now, on top of the 25 live, and Financial Finesse customers can offer our program to their employees in each one.

10 · What I learned

The biggest change wasn't the technology. It was how I design.

I no longer wait until something is scoped and scheduled to learn whether it has potential. I can recognize a problem, build a useful first version, put it in front of people, and improve it from what happens in practice. Moving fast doesn't remove the need for judgment: I still have to understand the workflow, decide what to automate, design for uncertainty and failure, and know when a person's expertise beats another AI response.

See the opportunity.

Find meaningful problems, even when nobody has put them on the roadmap.

Build to learn.

Make ideas tangible early, so real workflows shape the product instead of assumptions.

Design for trust.

Give AI real responsibility while keeping people informed and in control.

I don't just design what software could do. I build to find out what it should do. The most valuable part wasn't learning to code with AI. It was learning how much more I could contribute when strategy, design, user feedback, and implementation became one continuous process.
